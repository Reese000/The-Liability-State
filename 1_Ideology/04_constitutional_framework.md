## Purpose

This document establishes the **structural format** for the Liability State constitution — not the final text of every provision, but the framework each provision must follow. Individual constitutional provisions will be drafted as separate sections below and filled in over time as the system design matures.

---

## The Four-Part Structure

Every constitutional provision in the Liability State must contain the following four elements. This structure prevents the interpretive drift that has undermined other constitutions (see [Core Principles §8](./01_core_principles.md#8-constitutional-clarity--contextual-grounding)):

### 1. Problem Statement

Describes the specific problem this provision is designed to solve. Written in the present tense, grounded in the conditions of the founding era.

**Format**: "The constitution addresses the problem of [specific problem], which arises when [mechanism of the problem]."

### 2. Mechanism Description

Describes the mechanism the provision uses to solve the problem, and why that mechanism was chosen.

**Format**: "The constitution employs [mechanism] for the following reason: [justification]. The mechanism works as follows: [brief functional description]."

### 3. Expected Outcome

States the measurable result the provision is designed to produce. This serves as the interpretive anchor — courts applying the provision must be able to reference this outcome.

**Format**: "The intended outcome of this provision is [specific, measurable result]. Success is defined as [criteria]. Failure to achieve this outcome within [timeframe] shall trigger [review/revision mechanism]."

### 4. Historical Context (Preamble Section)

A separate preamble describes the problems and conditions of the era when the constitution was written. This provides future generations with the interpretive context needed to understand why particular mechanisms were chosen.

**Format**: A narrative section at the beginning of the constitution, written as a historical record.

---

## Purpose Transposition Rule

Courts interpreting constitutional provisions may extend the mechanism to **analogous problems of the same class** — but not to fundamentally different problems. The "Problem Statement" is the provision's teleological anchor.

A problem class is defined by:
- **The type of harm** (physical, economic, environmental, systemic)
- **The relationship between the parties** (individual-to-individual, individual-to-corporation, compact-to-compact)
- **The nature of the externality** (local, cross-boundary, global, temporal)

If a new problem matches all three dimensions of a provision's problem statement, the provision may be extended. If any dimension differs materially, a new constitutional provision is required.

### Glass Box Personal Mandate (Knowledge Boundary)

**Status**: Drafted
**Priority**: High

#### 1. Problem Statement

The constitution addresses the problem of **knowledge-driven surveillance**, which arises when the information infrastructure required for accurate liability assignment, insurance pricing, and judicial evidence collection enables systematic surveillance of citizens by insurers, courts, and data aggregators. A system designed to collect data for legitimate liability purposes can, without proper boundaries, become a surveillance apparatus that erodes privacy, enables social control, and concentrates informational power in entities with no democratic accountability.

#### 2. Mechanism Description

The constitution employs a **four-principle knowledge boundary** for the following reason: structural constraints on data collection are more robust than technological safeguards or promises of good behavior, because they change the incentives of data collectors rather than relying on their restraint.

The four principles are:

**Principle 1 — Burden of Proof on the Data Collector**: Any entity collecting personal data must demonstrate a direct causal link between each data point and a specific, quantifiable risk they are authorized to assess. Correlation alone is insufficient. If an insurer wants to price based on a behavioral factor, they must prove that the behavior causes the loss, not merely that it correlates with it.

**Principle 2 — Proactive Knowledge vs. Reactive Surveillance**: Information that a person or organization voluntarily produces in the normal course of operations (driving records, emissions logs, safety test results) is proactive knowledge and may be shared as a condition of insurance or commerce. Information collected about a person without their active participation (location tracking, behavioral monitoring, data inference) is reactive surveillance and requires a court warrant based on probable cause.

**Principle 3 — Minimum Knowledge Requirement**: Every data collection system must collect the minimum information necessary to achieve its stated purpose. The Actuarial Commons maintains published minimally sufficient data protocols for each line of insurance. No entity may demand more than the published minimum without demonstrating insufficiency to a private court.

**Principle 4 — Glass Box for Data**: All data collection protocols, scoring algorithms, and risk models must be published and auditable by the Actuarial Commons. Citizens have the right to know what data is collected about them, how it is weighted, and who has access. Secret scoring is a per se violation.

#### 3. Expected Outcome

The intended outcome of this provision is that the Liability State maintains sufficient knowledge for accurate liability assignment while preventing any single entity from accumulating a comprehensive surveillance apparatus. Success is defined as: (a) no insurer or data aggregator holds a dataset broad enough to profile citizens beyond specific insurance lines, (b) citizens retain the practical ability to conduct their lives without being continuously monitored, and (c) data collection is bounded by demonstrated causal necessity rather than speculative value. Failure to achieve this outcome within ten years of ratification shall trigger a constitutional review of data collection practices.

#### 4. Historical Context (Preamble)

The twenty-first century saw the emergence of surveillance capitalism, where companies and governments built comprehensive data empires by collecting information at scale under the guise of service optimization, security, and risk management. These data empires enabled social control, political manipulation, and systematic discrimination. The data itself became a liability — honeypots for hackers, tools for blackmail, and instruments of authoritarian governance. The Liability State's founders recognized that their own system required extensive knowledge to function, and chose to bound that knowledge architecturally rather than trusting data collectors to exercise restraint. They embedded these boundaries in the constitution so that no future efficiency argument could erode them without explicit democratic consent.

## Amendment Process

**Proposed rule**: Constitutional provisions may be amended only by:
1. A 75% supermajority of the House of Repeal, AND
2. Ratification by a majority of Regional Compacts

No amendment may remove or weaken the Problem Statement of any provision without replacing it with a new Problem Statement for the same problem class. This prevents silent drift — if a future generation wants to change the constitution, they must explicitly state what new problem they are solving.
